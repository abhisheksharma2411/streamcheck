Whether a streaming payment pipeline charges a customer twice after a restart turns on a comparison nobody performs: how long the provider remembers an idempotency key, against how stale a record can be when the pipeline processes it for the second time. The first number is in the provider's documentation. The second is not written anywhere -- it is assembled from a checkpoint interval in one file, a recovery assumption in a runbook, a restart delay in a third place and a source's lateness bound in a fourth. Each file is defensible alone, and the fault is in the relation between them. We give a checker that gathers those facts and states the relations, and a synthesiser that turns each finding into a runnable scenario: these records, kill the job here, bring it back after this long, and the provider will show two charges for one order. Across a corpus of 630 pipelines, 210 of 515 findings cannot be derived from fewer than three artifacts, and no single artifact yields more than 35 of the 305 duration findings. Executing the witnesses corrected our own rules: two were demoted after their scenarios produced a repeated call that the provider's idempotency absorbed rather than a second charge. On a real Kafka broker, witnesses reproduce the double charge, and a matched control in which the provider never forgets the key does not -- which is what makes the second charge attributable to the lifetime rather than to the replay. Fault injection finds the same defects only if the tester already assumed a long enough outage: one willing to simulate five minutes finds 15% of them, and 12 hours is needed for all. We also find a configuration with no application-level fix at all: above roughly a 14-minute recovery assumption, the transaction timeout required to survive it exceeds the broker's own cap.
